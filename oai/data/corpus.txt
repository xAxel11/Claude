the machine does not know anything when it starts. every number inside it is
random. it reads a window of characters and it guesses what comes next, and at
first the guess is worthless, because a random network has no reason to prefer
one letter over another. the only thing it has going for it is that we can
measure exactly how wrong it was, and we can push every number a little way in
the direction that would have made it less wrong.

that is the whole idea. do it once and nothing happens. do it a hundred
thousand times and the network begins to notice that a q is nearly always
followed by a u, that a space follows a period more often than not, that after
the letters t and h the letter e is a very good bet. none of this was
programmed. it fell out of counting mistakes.

we call the measure of wrongness the loss. when the model is guessing at random
across a vocabulary of about seventy symbols, the loss sits near the logarithm
of seventy, which is a little over four. that number is the price of total
ignorance. as the model learns, the loss falls. a loss of three means it has
learned something about which characters are common. a loss of two means it has
learned the shape of words. a loss near one means it has learned the shape of
sentences, and it is starting to remember phrases it has seen before.

the exponential of the loss is called the perplexity, and it has a nicer
meaning: it is roughly the number of characters the model is still choosing
between at each position. a perplexity of seventy is a coin toss with seventy
faces. a perplexity of four means it has narrowed the field down to about four
plausible letters, which is close to what a person does when reading a word
with the last letter covered up.

there are three pieces inside this model and each does a simple job.

the first is the embedding table. a character is not a number in any meaningful
sense. the letter a is not one less than the letter b in any way that helps.
so we give every character in the vocabulary its own small vector of numbers,
and we let training decide what those numbers should be. after a while
something interesting happens: the vectors for the vowels drift toward each
other, and the vectors for the digits form their own cluster, and the space
character ends up somewhere all by itself. nobody told the model that vowels
were a category. it discovered that they behave alike.

the second piece is the hidden layer. it takes the vectors for every character
in the window, laid end to end, and mixes them together into a new vector of
its own. the mixing is a matrix multiply followed by a squashing function, and
the squashing is what makes the whole thing more than a fancy average. without
it, stacking layers would gain you nothing at all, because a chain of linear
maps is just another linear map. the squashing bends the space, and a bent
space can separate things a flat one cannot.

the third piece is the output layer. it scores every character in the
vocabulary, and the softmax turns those scores into probabilities that add up
to one. the probability it assigns to the character that actually came next is
the thing we want to be large. the negative logarithm of that probability is
the loss for that example, and averaging over a batch gives the loss for the
step.

then comes the part everyone finds strange the first time. we want to know how
much each of the eighty thousand numbers in the network contributed to the
error. computing that one number at a time would take forever. instead we run
the whole computation backwards. the error at the output tells us the error at
the hidden layer, which tells us the error at the embeddings. every operation
in the forward pass has a partner in the backward pass, and the partner is
usually simpler than the original. this is backpropagation, and it is really
just the chain rule from calculus applied carefully and in the right order.

once we have the gradient, we take a step. the simplest rule is to subtract a
small multiple of the gradient from every parameter, and that works, but it is
slow and it wobbles. the optimizer used here keeps two running averages for
every parameter: one of the gradient itself, and one of the gradient squared.
the first is momentum, which carries the parameter through flat stretches. the
second is a per parameter scale, which shrinks the step where the gradient has
been large and noisy and lengthens it where the gradient has been small and
steady. together they make the descent much steadier than plain gradient
descent, and they cost only two extra numbers per parameter.

there is one more safeguard. sometimes a batch produces an enormous gradient,
usually because it contains something the model has never seen. if we take a
step proportional to that gradient we can undo an hour of progress in a single
update. so we measure the length of the whole gradient vector, and if it is
longer than a threshold we shrink it back down to the threshold while keeping
its direction. this is called gradient clipping and it is the difference
between a run that finishes and a run that explodes.

training in this program happens on its own thread. that is not an
optimization, it is what makes the thing usable. the loop checks a single
atomic flag at the top of every iteration, and when the flag is set it stops,
writes the weights to disk, and returns. cancelling costs you the batch that
was in flight and nothing else. you can stop it, look at what it has learned,
change the learning rate, and start it again, and it picks up exactly where it
left off, because the optimizer state is saved alongside the weights.

the interface shows two things at once. on the left is the learning feed: the
loss falling, the norm of the gradient, samples of what the model can write at
this moment, and the characters it currently believes are most likely to
follow a given prefix. on the right is a chat box. anything you type that is
not a command is fed into the model as a prompt, and the model continues it.
early in training the continuation is noise. later it is words. later still it
is sentences that are almost right, which is somehow more unsettling than the
noise.

about the graphics card. a graphics card is very good at multiplying matrices
and very bad at being shared. most programs take the whole device and hold it
until they are done. this one asks for a share and sticks to it. when the
driver supports splitting a device into pieces, it asks for a piece with the
requested fraction of the compute units and works only inside that piece; the
rest of the card carries on with whatever else it was doing. when the driver
does not support splitting, which is the common case on consumer hardware, it
measures how long each batch of work occupied the device and then deliberately
does nothing for a proportional stretch afterwards. over a few seconds the
occupancy settles at the fraction you asked for. the memory it allocates is
capped at the same fraction, and if the model would not fit inside that cap it
declines the device and runs on the processor instead.

the processor path is not an afterthought. the matrix multiplies are blocked so
that the pieces being multiplied stay in cache, and they are split across a
pool of worker threads that park on a condition variable when there is nothing
to do. on a four core machine the training loop runs roughly twice as fast with
the pool as without it, which is about what you should expect once memory
bandwidth is taken into account.

a word about corpora. the model learns the text you give it and nothing else.
give it a thousand characters and it will memorize them, and you will see the
training loss fall to almost nothing while the held out loss climbs, which is
the clearest picture of overfitting you are ever likely to get. give it a
megabyte of ordinary prose and it will learn spelling, spacing, punctuation,
and the rhythm of sentences, and it will invent words that do not exist but
that look like they should. that gap, between memorizing and generalizing, is
the central problem of the field, and you can watch it open and close in a
program small enough to read in an afternoon.

none of this is a large language model. there is no attention here, no
transformer, no pretraining on the open internet. it is a feedforward network
over a fixed window of characters, which is an architecture from the early two
thousands. it is here because everything in a modern system is present in it in
miniature: a representation that is learned rather than designed, a nonlinear
mixing layer, a probability distribution over a vocabulary, a loss that
measures surprise, gradients computed by running the computation backwards, and
an optimizer that decides how far to move. understand this and the larger
systems are a matter of scale and of clever architecture, not of a different
kind of magic.

the code is plain c. there is no framework, no build system beyond a makefile,
and no dependency that is not part of the standard library. the graphics
support is loaded at run time by name, so the same binary runs on a machine
with a card and a machine without one. the whole thing compiles in a couple of
seconds and produces one executable that you can copy to another computer and
run.

writing it in c was a choice with consequences. you have to allocate your own
memory and free it, and you have to think about which thread owns which piece
of state, and there is no library call that will differentiate a function for
you. in exchange you get to see every number that moves. when the loss does not
fall you cannot blame a layer you did not write, because you wrote all of them.
that is a good position to learn from, even if it is a poor position from which
to train something enormous.

a few practical notes for anyone reading the source. the tensor file holds the
matrix type and the small set of operations the model needs. the network file
holds the forward pass, the backward pass, the optimizer, and the code that
reads and writes checkpoints. the training file holds the loop and everything
it reports. the interface file holds a grid of cells, drawn into a back buffer
and compared against the front buffer so that only the characters that actually
changed are written to the terminal, which is why the display does not flicker
even while the feed is scrolling quickly.

if you want to change something, start with the model size. widen the hidden
layer and watch the loss fall further and the speed fall with it. lengthen the
window and watch it learn longer range structure, at the cost of more
parameters in the first layer than you probably expected. shrink the embedding
and watch it struggle to tell characters apart. every one of these knobs is a
command line flag, and every one of them teaches you something the moment you
turn it.

and if the loss stops falling, the usual causes are these, in order of how
often they are actually the problem: the learning rate is too high and the run
is bouncing around a minimum rather than descending into it; the learning rate
is too low and it is descending so slowly that nothing appears to happen; the
model is too small to represent what the text contains; the text is too small
and the model has already memorized it. the held out loss tells you which of
the last two you are looking at. when the training loss falls and the held out
loss rises, you have memorized. when both are flat and high, you are too small.

there is a particular pleasure in watching a program learn something you did
not teach it. the first time the samples stop being noise and start containing
real words, spelled correctly, with spaces in sensible places, it is difficult
not to feel that something has happened. nothing has happened, of course. a few
tens of thousands of floating point numbers have been nudged around by an
algorithm that has no idea what a word is. but the words are there on the
screen, and they were not there an hour ago, and they came out of counting
mistakes.
